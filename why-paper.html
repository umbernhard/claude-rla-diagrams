Risk-limiting audits from the ground up · Part 0: why paper

Why do audits check paper ballots?

Most votes in the United States are counted by machines, and machines run software. Software can have bugs, it can be set up wrong, and in the worst case someone can tamper with it. Looking at a machine's output can't tell you which of these happened, because the output is all the software lets you see.

An audit needs evidence the software never had a chance to touch. A paper ballot is that evidence: the voter marked it, or checked what was printed on it, before it went into the box. Researchers Ron Rivest and John Wack gave the idea a name, software independence. A voting system is software independent if a change or error in its software can't change the outcome without someone being able to notice.

The paper doesn't move

However many records the software changes, the paper stays where the voters left it. That's what makes the paper worth checking: it is a record of the vote that doesn't depend on the software being right.

Switch to "No paper" and count again. Now the only thing left to count is the electronic records, and recounting them gives back the same wrong answer. Without an independent record, a check can only ask the software whether the software was right.

People make mistakes too

A hand count is done by people, and people misread marks, lose their place, and put ballots in the wrong pile. So a hand count won't always match the paper exactly either.

What saves it is that honest mistakes are small and go in both directions. A tired counter is about as likely to misread a vote for Bob as a vote for Alice, so most mistakes cancel out. For counting mistakes to change who won, they have to pile up on one side by more than the margin. Try it below.

Real contests that are close enough for counting mistakes to matter usually trigger a recount, sometimes more than one, with careful procedures for disputed marks. And mistakes that all go one way aren't really mistakes anymore. That's tampering, which brings up the last piece.

Paper has to be protected

Paper is harder to change than software, but it isn't impossible. Someone with access to the ballots after the polls close could fill in an empty oval on a ballot where the voter skipped a contest, add ballots that were never cast, or make ballots disappear. A hand count of altered paper finds the altered result.

So the paper is only as trustworthy as the way it's handled. Election offices keep a chain of custody: a record of where the ballots are, who has had access to them, and when, from the moment they're cast until the audit is finished. Containers are sealed and logged, people work in bipartisan pairs, and the number of ballots is checked against the number of voters who signed in. A later page will look at chain of custody in more detail.

Where this leaves us

With paper the voters checked and a sound chain of custody, a full hand count finds the outcome the voters chose, however the electronic records were changed. That makes the hand count the gold standard for checking an election.

It's also a lot of work. A hand count of a statewide contest means teams of people reading millions of ballots, for days or weeks, and most of the time it just confirms what the machines already said.
