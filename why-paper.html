Risk-limiting audits from the ground up · Part 0: why paper

Why do audits check paper ballots?

Most votes in the United States are counted by machines, and machines run software. Software can have bugs, it can be set up wrong, and in the worst case someone can tamper with it. Looking at a machine's output can't tell you which of these happened, because the output is all the software lets you see.

An audit needs evidence the software never had a chance to touch. A paper ballot is that evidence: the voter marked it, or checked what was printed on it, before it went into the box. Researchers Ron Rivest and John Wack gave the idea a name, software independence. A voting system is software independent if a change or error in its software can't change the outcome without someone being able to notice.

The paper doesn't move

However many records the software changes, the paper stays where the voters left it. That's what makes the paper worth auditing: it is a record of the vote that doesn't depend on the software being right.

Switch to "No paper" and try again. Now every record you pull agrees with the count, because the count was made from those same records. Without an independent record, a check can only ask the software whether the software was right.

Where this leaves us

To get past the audit, a change to the electronic records has to dodge every paper ballot the audit pulls. The more records you change, the harder that is, and changing the outcome takes a lot of changed records when the margin is wide. The rest of the series is about the other side of the bargain: how many paper ballots an audit needs to check so that a wrong outcome would almost certainly be caught, while a right one can be confirmed without counting everything.

One thing has to be true for any of this to work. The paper has to be a trustworthy record of the vote, which means voters marked or checked it, and the boxes are kept secure until the audit. An audit is only as good as the paper it checks.
