Risk-limiting audits from the ground up · Part 0: why paper

Why do audits check paper ballots?

Most votes in the United States are counted by machines, and machines run software. Software can have bugs, it can be set up wrong, and in the worst case someone can tamper with it. Looking at a machine's output can't tell you which of these happened, because the output is all the software lets you see.

An audit needs evidence the software never had a chance to touch. A paper ballot is that evidence: the voter marked it, or checked what was printed on it, before it went into the box. (Both ways have weaknesses; more on that below.) Ron Rivest and John Wack gave the idea a name, software independence: "A voting system is software independent if an (undetected) change or error in its software cannot cause an undetectable change or error in an election outcome."1

Why not trust the software?

Start with something that's true of all software, not just voting machines: it has bugs, and some of those bugs are security vulnerabilities. Even reading a program's source code can't settle the question. In a famous 1984 lecture, Ken Thompson showed how a tampered compiler, the program that turns source code into a working program, could slip a hidden back door into everything it builds, including new copies of itself, while the source code stays clean.2 Operating systems, phones and web browsers get security updates so often because nobody knows how to write a large program that's free of flaws, and anything connected to the outside world gives attackers a way to look for them. Voting systems are no different. If anything, the research suggests that historically they've been worse than most, with basic security mistakes that other industries had learned to avoid.

Researchers have shown, again and again, that voting software can be made to lie. In 2004, a review of leaked source code for a widely used touchscreen voting machine found flaws that would let voters cast extra ballots and let insiders change votes without being detected.3 In 2005, Harri Hursti changed an optical scanner's results using nothing but its memory card, a finding that a review for California's Secretary of State confirmed.4

In 2007, California and Ohio each commissioned teams of researchers to examine the voting systems in use in their states. California's red teams concluded that all three systems they tested could be compromised,5 and Ohio's teams reported that they were able to "subvert every voting system" they were given.6 The same year, another team showed that someone with a minute of access to a touchscreen machine or its memory card could install software that steals votes, alters the machine's records to match, and spreads like a virus to other machines during normal election procedures.7 Later studies found similar problems in machines used in New Jersey89 and India,10 and in equipment examined at public hacking events.11 In 2022, the federal Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency published nine vulnerabilities in a touchscreen ballot-marking device, found by two of these researchers.12

None of these studies showed that a real election had been changed. They show something more basic: there's no practical way to prove that the software in a voting machine is correct and hasn't been altered. So the evidence that an outcome is right has to come from somewhere the software can't reach. In 2018, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine recommended that all U.S. elections use human-readable paper ballots and that states require risk-limiting audits before certifying results.13

The paper doesn't move

However many records the software changes, the paper stays where the voters left it. That's what makes the paper worth checking: it is a record of the vote that doesn't depend on the software being right.

People make mistakes too

A hand count is done by people, and people misread marks, lose their place, and put ballots in the wrong pile. So a hand count won't always match the paper exactly either. In a 2012 lab study, Stephen Goggin, Michael Byrne and Juan Gilbert had groups of three or four volunteers count stacks of 120 mock ballots, using counting procedures that real states use. Nearly half the groups got at least one candidate's count wrong. The errors were small, though: on average about 1.4% of the ballots, ranging from about 0.5% with the more structured procedure to about 2% with the other.14 A study of two statewide recounts in Wisconsin found the original tallies and the recounts differed by a fraction of a percent, and that paper ballots counted by scanners were more accurate than paper ballots counted by hand.15

What saves it is that honest mistakes are small and go in both directions. A tired counter is about as likely to misread a vote for Bob as a vote for Alice, so most mistakes cancel out. When researchers re-counted about 47,000 ballots from New Mexico's 2006 general election in Bernalillo County, the hand counts differed from the machine count by less than 0.2% on average, and the differences didn't favor any candidate or party. Every batch was hand counted twice, and even the two hand counts didn't always agree: depending on how the ballots were cast, between 45% and 70% or more of batches came out identical. But no batch was off by more than 6 ballots, and the differences canceled out across candidates.16 Counting procedures also catch many slips before they reach the final tally. In Rhode Island's 2019 audit pilots, teams recounted whenever two tallies disagreed, and one team redid a contest after mixing up its stacks. The final hand counts matched the original results exactly.17 For counting mistakes to change who won, they have to pile up on one side by more than the margin. Try it below.

Contests close enough for counting mistakes to matter are the ones that get recounted, with careful procedures for disputed marks. A study of New Hampshire recounts from 1946 to 2002 estimated that a race decided by less than about half a percent has a real chance of flipping on a recount, and suggested treating 0.5% as a minimum threshold for automatic recounts.18 And mistakes that all go one way aren't really mistakes anymore. That's tampering, and we'll come back to it after a closer look at the ballots themselves.

Every kind of ballot has trade-offs

Paper only helps if it records what the voter meant, and no way of marking a ballot gets that right every time. Hand-marked paper ballots are error-prone. In laboratory studies where researchers knew exactly what each voter intended, people made mistakes on about 1–2% of their choices on paper bubble ballots.1920 In real elections, some voters mark too many candidates in a contest, an overvote, and then none of their choices in that contest count. After New York's 2010 election, the Brennan Center estimated that more than 60,000 votes were lost this way statewide.21 A NIST study of ballots from a 2009 California election found that about 4.9% of marks were "marginal": check marks, crosses, and partial or light fills that a scanner can't reliably classify.22

Recent audits of ballot images show what this looks like in practice. Georgia now audits images of every ballot after statewide elections. Across the November 2024 general election, the November 2025 municipal elections and the 2026 primaries, auditors flagged 186 of about 365,000 hand-marked ballots, roughly 1 in 2,000, where the votes as counted and the audit's reading of the voter's marks disagreed. Usually the marks were unclear enough that county reviewers and auditors read them differently; some were faint marks the original count missed. Among about 9.7 million printed ballots, the same audits found 18 discrepancies, all traced to configuration, tabulation or adjudication problems.232425 In South Carolina's June 2026 primary, all 31 discrepancies the audit found were on hand-marked ballots, even though those were only about 14,000 of the 855,000 ballots cast. Most were marks the scanner missed that auditors judged to be clear votes.26

Ballot-marking devices, touchscreens that print the voter's choices onto paper, avoid some of these mistakes: they can stop a voter from choosing too many candidates and warn about contests left blank. They have quirks of their own, though. In South Carolina's audit, 21 of about 841,000 printed ballots had no readable list of the voter's choices, even though the scanners had accepted and counted their barcodes.26

The trade-off is that a printed ballot is only as trustworthy as the voter's check of it. In a realistic mock election where the printouts were secretly altered, voters left to themselves rarely caught the change: about 40% looked at their printout, and fewer than 1 in 10 reported a problem. Simple steps at the polling place made a large difference. When a poll worker told voters that the paper was the official record, more than 90% looked at their ballot. When voters also brought a list of the candidates they planned to vote for, most noticed the altered ballot: in the strongest version, 71% reported it on an exit survey and 86% told a poll worker.27 How far the paper from ballot-marking devices can be trusted depends on how they're used, and researchers still debate it.28

Scanned pictures of ballots aren't a substitute for the paper either. Many scanners save an image of every ballot they count, and it's tempting to audit the images, which are easy to share and search. But the images are made by software too. Researchers showed that malicious code in a scanner driver or election computer can alter the marks in ballot images so they show votes for a different candidate, and concluded that audits have to look at the physical ballots.29

Paper has to be protected

Paper is harder to change than software, but it isn't impossible. Someone with access to the ballots after the polls close could fill in an empty oval on a ballot where the voter skipped a contest, add ballots that were never cast, or make ballots disappear. A hand count of altered paper finds the altered result. Handling mistakes happen too: in Wisconsin's 2016 presidential recount, local officials' minutes cited lost ballots in 23 wards and ballots found during the recount in 72.30

So the paper is only as trustworthy as the way it's handled. Election offices keep a chain of custody: a record of where the ballots are, who has had access to them, and when, from the moment they're cast until the audit is finished. Containers are sealed and logged, ballots change hands only with more than one person present, and the number of ballots is reconciled against the number of voters who signed in.31 Checking that these steps were actually followed is sometimes called a compliance audit, and it comes before any count of the paper.32 A later page will look at chain of custody in more detail.

This is an idealized picture

The steps above describe chain of custody in general terms. The actual rules are set by state law and by each election office's policies, and they look different from place to place. Take "more than one person present": depending on the jurisdiction, that can mean the two people handing ballots off, those two plus additional observers, or workers from different political parties. Which containers get sealed, how seals are logged, and how ballots are counted and reconciled vary in the same way.

That variation is normal, and usually fine. What an audit depends on is that the jurisdiction has written procedures, follows them, and keeps records that show it did.

No chain of custody is perfect, and none ever will be. Elections are run by people, often thousands of temporary poll workers working long days, and people make mistakes: a seal number gets copied down wrong, a transfer is logged late, a bag of ballots turns up in the wrong room. Most of these lapses are innocent, and like the counting mistakes above, many of them don't favor any one candidate and tend to cancel out.

But not all mistakes are created equal. Misreading a ballot here and there moves a few votes in both directions. Losing a batch of ballots moves all of them at once, and if those voters leaned one way, so does the error. In the November 2025 mayoral election in Hamtramck, Michigan, 37 absentee ballots were never taken out of their envelopes and counted on election night, which officials blamed on human error at the counting board.33 The ballots turned up in the clerk's office a few days later. But several city officials had been in the office in the meantime, which broke the chain of custody, and the county canvassers deadlocked over whether to count them. The certified result gave Adam Alharbi the win by 11 votes. After nearly a year in court, the ballots were counted in October 2026 and put Muhith Mahmood ahead by 12 in an unofficial count.34 One mistake was enough to change the outcome. The broken custody is what kept the ballots out of the count, and the result in doubt, for most of a year.

That also tells us where to look first. A single lapse affects the most ballots at the points where whole containers change hands or sit unattended: ballots moving from polling places and drop boxes to the counting center, absentee ballots that have been received but not yet counted, and ballots in storage between election night and the audit. Those are the places to tighten first. Every gap in the record, large or small, is also a gap in the evidence that the paper being audited is the paper voters cast, and from the outside an innocent lapse can look the same as tampering. So election offices should keep custody as tight as they can, write down what happened when something goes wrong, and treat those records as part of the evidence rather than as paperwork.

Where this leaves us

With paper the voters checked and a sound chain of custody, a full hand count finds the outcome the voters chose, however the electronic records were changed. That makes the hand count the gold standard for checking an election.

It's also a lot of work. A hand count of a statewide contest means teams of people reading millions of ballots, for days or weeks, and most of the time it just confirms what the machines already said.
