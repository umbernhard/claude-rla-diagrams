# "What elections actually are": planning notes

Started 2026-10-25. A future page (the author plans to write it himself) about how elections are messy, human processes that can never be a perfect record of "the will of the people," whatever that means. It also frames what an audit can and can't claim.

Related changes already made: the sampling page now says a hand count "is treated as representative of" the outcome voters chose, and why-paper's closing says a hand count "is the closest anyone can get" to it, since no count captures every voter's intent perfectly.

## Outline

1. **Preferences aren't fixed.** People give different answers when asked the same question again; survey answers are partly constructed on the spot.
2. **"The will of the people" isn't well defined.** With three or more options, no counting rule satisfies every reasonable fairness condition, and different rules can pick different winners from the same ballots.
3. **The ballot doesn't always record what the voter meant.** Ballot design, voter mistakes on paper and on BMDs, marginal marks. Mostly uses sources already on the why-paper page.
4. **Not everyone who wants to vote gets to.** Voter ID (the evidence is contested), long lines and who waits in them, voter-roll and check-in errors.
5. **What an audit can and can't claim.** A tabulation audit checks that the ballots that were cast were counted right. That's narrower than "the result reflects the will of the people."

Overlaps with the combined-uncertainty and holistic-audit items in `notes/sampling.md`; sections 3 and 4 are most of that calculation's inputs, so the calculation could live on this page.

## Sources

Checked against Crossref records on 2026-10-25 unless marked otherwise. None are in `bib/references.json` yet; add them when the page is written.

**1. Unstable preferences**
- Philip E. Converse, "The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics," in David Apter (ed.), *Ideology and Discontent*, Free Press, 1964. Reprinted in *Critical Review* 18(1–3):1–74, 2006, doi:10.1080/08913810608443650. The "nonattitudes" argument: many people's answers to the same policy question change from one survey wave to the next. (Original book chapter not checked; the reprint was.)
- Christopher H. Achen, "Mass Political Attitudes and the Survey Response," *American Political Science Review* 69(4):1218–1231, 1975, doi:10.2307/1955282. Argues much of that instability is measurement error in the questions rather than the absence of real opinions.
- John Zaller and Stanley Feldman, "A Simple Theory of the Survey Response: Answering Questions versus Revealing Preferences," *American Journal of Political Science* 36(3):579–616, 1992, doi:10.2307/2111583. People answer by sampling whatever considerations are on their mind at the moment.

**2. Aggregation and "the will of the people"**
- Kenneth J. Arrow, "A Difficulty in the Concept of Social Welfare," *Journal of Political Economy* 58(4):328–346, 1950, doi:10.1086/256963. The impossibility theorem; expanded in *Social Choice and Individual Values* (Wiley, 1951; book not checked).
- William H. Riker, *Liberalism Against Populism: A Confrontation Between the Theory of Democracy and the Theory of Social Choice*, W.H. Freeman, 1982. (Book not in Crossref; title confirmed from a 1983 review by Gordon Tullock, doi:10.1332/251569298x15668907116526.)

**3. Ballots that don't record intent** (in addition to why-paper's sources: Byrne et al., Brennan 2011 overvotes, NIST marginal marks, Georgia/SC image audits, Bernhard et al. BMD study)
- Lawrence Norden, David Kimball, Whitney Quesenbery and Margaret Chen, *Better Ballots*, Brennan Center for Justice, 2008, https://www.brennancenter.org/media/234/download (found by web search; authors not yet checked against the document).
- Already in the bibliography: Wand et al. 2001 (butterfly ballot), Kimball & Kropf 2005 (ballot design and unrecorded votes), Ansolabehere & Stewart 2005 (residual votes by technology), Frisina et al. 2008 (Sarasota undervotes).

**4. Barriers to voting**
- Zoltan Hajnal, Nazita Lajevardi and Lindsay Nielson, "Voter Identification Laws and the Suppression of Minority Votes," *Journal of Politics* 79(2):363–379, 2017, doi:10.1086/688343. Finds strict ID laws lower minority turnout.
- Justin Grimmer, Eitan Hersh, Marc Meredith, Jonathan Mummolo and Clayton Nall, "Obstacles to Estimating Voter ID Laws' Effect on Turnout," *Journal of Politics* 80(3):1045–1051, 2018, doi:10.1086/696618. Shows the Hajnal et al. estimates aren't robust to data problems.
- Enrico Cantoni and Vincent Pons, "Strict ID Laws Don't Stop Voters: Evidence from a U.S. Nationwide Panel, 2008–2018," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 136(4):2615–2660, 2021, doi:10.1093/qje/qjab019. Finds no significant effect on turnout. Present ID as genuinely contested.
- Charles Stewart III and Stephen Ansolabehere, "Waiting to Vote," *Election Law Journal* 14(1):47–53, 2015, doi:10.1089/elj.2014.0292.
- Stephen Pettigrew, "The Racial Gap in Wait Times: Why Minority Precincts Are Underserved by Local Election Officials," *Political Science Quarterly* 132(3):527–547, 2017, doi:10.1002/polq.12657.
- Already in the bibliography: Edelstein & Edelstein 2010 (queuing), Buell 2013 (Richland County lines), Stewart 2019 (Elections Performance Index), Alvarez et al. 2009 (voter-roll matching), Elections Group 2023 (holistic audits).
