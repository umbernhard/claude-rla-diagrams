Risk-limiting audits from the ground up · Part 2: ballot polling

Ballot landscapes

After an election, how do we know the votes were counted correctly? A risk-limiting audit checks by reading a random sample of the paper ballots by hand. If the sample agrees strongly enough with the reported winner, the audit stops. If it doesn't, the audit keeps going, all the way to a full hand count if it has to. The risk limit, written α (the Greek letter alpha), is the largest chance we're willing to accept that a wrong outcome slips through and an audit ends before counting all the ballots.

This page builds up the reasoning behind one kind of audit, a ballot polling audit, from a single picture. Lay an election's ballots out in some order and you can draw them as a path: one step right for each ballot with a vote for Alice, one step up for each ballot with a vote for Bob. Invalid ballots, ones that don't contain a vote for either candidate, don't move the path. Stack every possible path on top of each other and you get what we'll call a ballot landscape. An audit is a walk across that landscape, one ballot at a time.

Before any audit: every possible election

Let's start with a small election to make things easy for now: six ballots, each with a vote for Alice or Bob. To keep things simple, we'll leave out invalid ballots for now; they don't move the path, so they wouldn't change the picture. The first ballot can be for Alice or for Bob: two possibilities. Each of those can be followed by a ballot with a vote for Alice or for Bob, which makes four, and every further ballot doubles the count again. Six ballots give 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 26 = 64 possible sequences, from AAAAAA to BBBBBB. Before we've read a single ballot, any sequence of ballots, and therefore any outcome, is possible. Read ballots one at a time and watch which possible outcomes survive.

To say how likely any outcome is, we first have to decide how much each possibility counts, and there are two natural choices. We could treat every sequence of ballots as equally likely, which is like assuming each ballot was a fair coin flip. In that case the landscape behaves like a Galton board, the pegboard where balls bounce left or right off each peg and pile up into a bell curve at the bottom. Each ballot is one bounce, right for Alice and up for Bob, and the number of ways to reach each point comes straight from Pascal's triangle. Or we could treat every final result as equally likely, from Alice receiving 0 votes (and Bob getting all 6) through Alice receiving all 6 (and Bob getting none), and split each result's share evenly among the sequences that produce it. The difference matters for auditing. If every sequence is equally likely, reading one ballot tells us nothing about the underlying population of ballots: the unread ones are still coin flips. If every result is equally likely, each ballot we read does tell us something about the rest: results with more Alice votes have more of their sequences start with Alice, so if our first sample is a ballot for Alice, it's more likely that the outcome is one in which Alice wins. From possible outcomes to an audit

Both pictures above needed a decision before we read a single ballot: how likely each result is to begin with. Change that starting guess and the chance that Alice won changes with it. In our every-sequence-is-equally-likely case, we assumed each ballot was equally likely to be a vote for Alice or a vote for Bob, but someone else could just as well assume that every ballot was a vote for Alice, or that every ballot was a vote for Bob. In our every-result-is-equally-likely case, we gave each of the seven possible results the same weight, but someone could just as easily assume that only one result is possible. An audit can't rest on a guess like that, because anyone who reasonably doubts the result could reasonably choose a different one.

A risk-limiting audit avoids the problem by turning the question around. Instead of asking how likely is each result, given our sample?, it asks how likely is our sample, given each result? That second question needs no starting guess. Pick a result, and its sequences are fixed: we can count what share of them begin the way our sample did.

We only need two results. The first is the reported result, the one we're checking. The second is the closest result in which the reported winner didn't win: a tie.1 For each of these two results, we look at all of its sequences and ask what share of them start out like our sample: the same number of votes for Alice and for Bob among the first ballots read. On the picture, that's the share of paths passing through the point our sample has reached. Dividing the reported result's share by the tie's share gives the likelihood ratio: how many times more likely our sample is if the reported result is right than if the election was really a tie. In the six-ballot election below, suppose the first two ballots both have a vote for Alice. Of the 15 sequences for a 4–2 Alice win, 6 start that way (40%). Of the 20 sequences for a 3–3 tie, only 4 do (20%). So the likelihood ratio is 40% ÷ 20% = 2: a start like ours is twice as likely if Alice really won.

The audit keeps reading ballots until that ratio reaches one divided by the risk limit (1/α). For a 5% risk limit, α = 0.05 and 1/α = 20. The risk we're limiting is that the reported winner didn't really win, yet the audit stops anyway and confirms them. Why does stopping at 1/α keep that risk below α?

Suppose the result really is a tie. Then each ballot's effect on the ratio is like a fair bet, one where you expect to break even. Think of betting on coin flips at even odds: a bet on any single flip, betting that a coin comes up heads or tails, might win or lose, but on average your money stays where it is. In the same way, a vote for Alice pushes the ratio up and a vote for Bob pushes it down, and when the truth is a tie those pushes balance out on average.

Fair bets can still leave you ahead by luck, but not reliably: if you start with $1 and can never bet more than you have, the chance you go on a winning streak and reach $20 is at most 1 in 20. The 20s aren't a coincidence. Because every bet is fair, your average bankroll stays at $1 no matter how you play, even if you quit the moment you hit $20. If you reached $20 more than 1 time in 20, those wins alone would average out to more than $1, and since you can never drop below $0, nothing could pull the average back down. The same reasoning says the chance of reaching $18 is at most 1 in 18, and in general the chance of turning $1 into $c is at most 1 in c. The ratio works the same way: it starts at 1, and it can only grow twentyfold by luck at most 1 time in 20.2 So when the truth is a tie, the audit wrongly stops at most 5% of the time; otherwise it keeps going, all the way to a full hand count if it has to.

If Bob actually won, samples that favor Alice are even rarer, so the risk is smaller still. That's why comparing against a tie is enough. This is the idea behind BRAVO, the standard method for ballot polling audits.3

If the ballots really are a tie, every confirmation is a mistake, and over many audits the share of mistakes settles at or below the risk limit α. In the default 600–400 election with α = 0.10, about 1 audit in 11 goes wrong, so ten runs show at least one failure more often than not. If the result is flipped, mistakes almost never happen: the evidence runs the wrong way, and the audit keeps going until a full hand count finds Bob's win. This is the ratio from step 4, tracked ballot by ballot on a log scale. Each vote for Alice multiplies it by (votes for Alice left in the reported election) ÷ (votes for Alice left in the tie), votes for Bob do the same with Bob's counts, and invalid ballots leave it alone. The audit stops when it reaches log(1/α). The dashed line is BRAVO's version, (2p)a(2−2p)b, which treats each ballot as if it were drawn with replacement.3

Notes

  1. Which tie: the page's tie keeps the reported number of invalid ballots and splits the valid ones evenly. When the valid count is odd, Bob gets the extra vote, which is the closest result in which Alice doesn't win. Why the tie is enough: Each vote for Alice multiplies the ratio by (votes for Alice left in the reported result) ÷ (votes for Alice left in the tie). The reported result has more votes for Alice, so this factor is always at least 1. Likewise each vote for Bob multiplies it by a factor that is at most 1. Now take any result in which Alice doesn't win and that has the same number of invalid ballots. It can be made from the tie by turning some votes for Alice into votes for Bob. That trades factors of at least 1 for factors of at most 1, so at every step the expected change in the ratio can only go down. Under a tie the ratio is a fair bet; under any of these results it's a bet tilted against reaching 1/α, so a wrong stop still happens at most α of the time. In technical terms, the ratio is a supermartingale under every such result, and Ville's inequality (note 2) still applies. Results with a different number of invalid ballots need more care; BRAVO avoids the question by setting invalid ballots aside. ↩
  2. In mathematical terms: if the result is really a tie, the likelihood ratio is a nonnegative martingale that starts at 1, and Ville's inequality says the chance it ever reaches 1/α is at most α. See J. Ville, Étude critique de la notion de collectif (Gauthier-Villars, 1939). Stopping as soon as the likelihood ratio crosses a threshold is Wald's sequential probability ratio test: A. Wald, “Sequential Tests of Statistical Hypotheses,” Annals of Mathematical Statistics 16(2), 1945. ↩
  3. M. Lindeman, P. B. Stark and V. S. Yates, “BRAVO: Ballot-polling Risk-limiting Audits to Verify Outcomes,” EVT/WOTE ’12, 2012.
