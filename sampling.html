Risk-limiting audits from the ground up · Part 1: sampling

What can a few ballots tell you?

After an election, officials report a winner. An audit checks that report against the paper ballots. A full hand count of the paper is treated as representative of the outcome the voters chose, but it means reading every ballot, which is slow and expensive. A risk-limiting audit reads a random sample of ballots instead, and stops as soon as the sample makes a strong enough case.

New to audits? Start with why audits check paper ballots.

This page is about the first half of that idea: why a random handful of ballots can speak for the whole box, and what it can and can't say. It treats the election as a statistical object, a fixed box of ballots, and assumes the sample is a fair random draw from it. How real audits make that true is the next part.

An election, drawn as a picture

Every example on this page uses the simplest contest there is: two candidates, Alice and Bob, and each voter votes for one of them. Real ballots usually list many contests, and some contests let voters choose more than one candidate, so a ballot and a vote aren't the same thing. Here we follow just this one contest, and every ballot has exactly one vote in it. When the counting is done, the result is two numbers: how many votes Alice got, and how many Bob got.

Two numbers make a point on a grid, so we can draw any result as a place: Alice's count across, Bob's count up. Counting builds the picture one ballot at a time. Each ballot with a vote for Alice is one step to the right, and each ballot with a vote for Bob is one step up.

Counting ten ballots

Ten voters cast ten ballots. Count them one by one and watch where the steps lead. The diagonal splits the grid in two: below it Alice has more votes, above it Bob does, and on it the election is tied. Every ten-ballot result lands on the dashed line where the two counts add up to ten.

There is no order of voting

Counting in a different order draws a different path, but every path ends at the same result. Ten ballots, six with a vote for Alice and four with a vote for Bob, can be put in 210 different orders, and here they all are at once.

Every order of the same ten ballots

Each one of these paths represents the same election outcome: 6 for Alice, 4 for Bob. Lines are thicker where more orders pass, and the number at each point counts how many of the 210 orders go through it. They all start at (0, 0) and all end at (6, 4). The steps toward Alice tend to be thicker because she got more votes: 126 of the 210 orders start with a vote for Alice and only 84 with a vote for Bob. At every point, the orders leaving it split in proportion to the votes for Alice and for Bob still left to count.

So which of these paths is the election? None of them in particular, or all of them at once. Every one ends at the same result, and nothing about the election picks one out. It's tempting to picture an audit as replaying the election ballot by ballot, in the order people voted. There is no such order. Two people can mark their ballots at the same moment. Ballots from different polling places are handled in different rooms, and mail ballots arrive over weeks.

None of that matters to the audit. Once the polls close, what's left is a pile of paper whose count no longer changes. The auditor creates an order by taking ballots out one at a time. Every sequence on this page is an order of taking ballots out, never an order of voting.

If the auditor counted every ballot, the order still wouldn't matter: every path ends at the same result. But the whole point of an audit like this is to avoid reading every ballot where possible. An auditor who stops partway sees only the start of one path, and the start of a path can be far from where it ends. So order does matter, but not in the way you might expect. The sequence itself doesn't carry any information. What matters is that the order decides which ballots the auditor has read by the time they stop, and the auditor treats those ballots as a fair sample of the whole box.

For the rest of this page, assume they are: every set of ballots is equally likely to be the one read. That assumption doesn't hold by itself. A box stacked one precinct after another can mislead a partial read badly, and making a draw that's fair, and that others can check, is the subject of the next part.

What a random sample looks like

An auditor reads ballots until the sample makes a strong enough case. How much is enough? Before an auditor can judge an unknown box from a sample, it helps to see what samples look like when we already know what's in the box.

Go back to the picture of every order of the same ballots. A fair shuffle makes each of those orders equally likely, so the share of paths passing through a point is the chance that a sample lands there. Counting paths is the same as working out chances. The next figure starts with a box small enough to count every path by hand. The one after it scales up to see the pattern that makes sampling work: a small sample can land almost anywhere, but the more ballots you read, the closer it sits to what's in the box.

Six ballots, every possible order

If we have an election with six ballots that Alice won with four votes to Bob's two, we can list every order a fair shuffle could produce. There are 15, each equally likely. Lines are thicker where more orders pass, and the number at each point counts the orders that go through it.

Move the slider to choose how many ballots have been read. The shaded diagonal marks every point an order can be at after that many ballots, and the chart on the right stands that diagonal up as bars: one bar for each point, as tall as the number of orders passing through it. Because every order is equally likely, the bars also show the chances of each possible sample.

A box of 100, shuffled 200 times

This is the same picture as the six-ballot example, just with more ballots, closer to the size of a real audit. A box of 100 can be put in far too many orders to draw them all (more than 1028, a 1 followed by 28 zeros), so instead the figure draws 200 of them at random. Each faint path is one shuffle of a box of 100 ballots: 60 with a vote for Alice and 40 with a vote for Bob. The darker path is a single shuffle, the kind one audit actually walks. Move the slider to choose how many ballots have been read. The dots on the gray diagonal show where each shuffle stands at that point.

The chart on the right works like the six-ballot one: each bar counts how many of the 200 shuffles hold that many votes for Alice at that point. The black line shows what those counts would be on average if we could use every possible order instead of 200 random ones, so it shows the exact chances. The bars wobble around the line because 200 shuffles is itself a sample; press Shuffle again and they wobble differently, while the line stays put. The shaded band covers the middle 90% of the chances.

The spread in Alice's vote count grows at first, then shrinks: near the end there are few ballots left to vary, and after all 100 every shuffle lands on exactly 60. As a share of the ballots read, the spread shrinks the whole way. The more you read, the closer the sample sits to 60%.

Put the ballot back, or set it aside?

The same picture can be turned on its corner. Put the start at the top and let every step fall downward: a vote for Alice falls to the right, a vote for Bob to the left. Each point on the grid becomes a peg, and a random sample becomes a ball bouncing down through the pegs into a bin at the bottom. This is a Galton board, also called a bean machine, after Francis Galton, who used one to show how many small random bounces pile up into a predictable shape. Each bin holds the samples with the same number of votes for Alice, so the pile of balls is the bar chart from the last two figures, built one sample at a time.

The board also shows a choice every audit has to make: what happens to a ballot after it's read. The figures so far set each ballot aside, so the box shrinks as the sample grows. Some well-known audit methods, including BRAVO, SuperSimple and MACRO, put it back instead.123 Every draw then picks from the whole box, and the same ballot can come up more than once.

Two boards, one box

Both boards draw from the same box of 20 ballots: 12 with a vote for Alice and 8 with a vote for Bob. At every peg a ball goes right for a vote for Alice or left for a vote for Bob, and the line leaving a peg is thicker on the side it's more likely to take. On the left board each ballot goes back into the box after it's read, so every peg has the same odds: 12 in 20 for Alice. On the right board each ballot is set aside, so the odds depend on what's already been drawn. After two votes for Alice, 10 of the 18 ballots left are for Alice. After two for Bob, 12 of 18 are.

Press Drop 100 to send 100 samples down each board. The bars count where the balls land, and the black line shows the exact chances, scaled to the number of balls. Both boards use the same scale, so the two piles can be compared directly.

Setting ballots aside makes the pile narrower, because every draw that leans one way leaves the box leaning the other. The difference depends on how much of the box the sample takes. Slide the sample to all 20 ballots: set aside, every ball lands on 12, because the sample is the whole box. Put back, it isn't. Some ballots get read twice and others never. Slide it down to 1 or 2 and the boards look almost alike. A real audit samples a few hundred ballots from thousands or millions, so the two piles are nearly the same, and either way of drawing works. The math is a little simpler when ballots go back, and a little more efficient when they're set aside.

Turning the question around

So far we knew what was in the box and asked what a sample would look like. An auditor has the opposite problem: they hold a sample and want to know what's in the box. To keep things simple, imagine the whole election is a single box of 1,000 ballots. A real election spreads its ballots across many containers, but once they're listed on a manifest and numbered, drawing from them works just like drawing from one big box.

To work out what's in the box, the auditor can use the same counting as the last two figures, where every order of the ballots was equally likely, just run backwards. Each possible result of the election is a different box: 600 votes for Alice and 400 for Bob, or 550 and 450, or a tie, and so on. For each of those possible boxes, ask: if the election really came out that way, how often would a random sample of the same size look like the one we're holding? A result that often produces samples like ours stays in the running. A result that almost never does starts to look doubtful. Each possible result is checked on its own, so there's no need to guess beforehand which results were likely.

To answer that, it helps to see how random samples behave when we do know what's in the box. Mostly, they look like the box. A box that's half votes for Alice gives samples that are around half votes for Alice. A box that's nearly all votes for Alice gives samples that are nearly all votes for Alice. Now and then, though, a sample comes out lopsided anyway, with far more votes for Alice than the box would usually give, or far fewer. That's what makes a sample surprising: it doesn't look like the box it came from.

Random samples look like the box

Pick an election and a sample size, then draw random samples. The squares show every ballot in the box, with the latest sample picked out. Each sample also gets its own row. For samples of 10, the row shows the ballots with the votes for Alice grouped first, so you can read the mix at a glance. Bigger samples are shown as a bar of Alice's share, with a line marking her share of the box. A row is marked surprising when a sample that far from the box, in that direction, would turn up less than 1 time in 40. A sample can be surprising in either direction, so all together, surprising samples turn up less than 1 time in 20.

Sample size

Switch elections and the samples change with the box. From the tie, most samples of 10 hold 4, 5 or 6 votes for Alice. From the 980 box, nearly every one is all Alice, and two votes for Bob is the surprise. Then switch to bigger samples: the bars crowd closer to the line. With 10 ballots from the tie, 7 for Alice (70%) is nothing special. With 200, a sample 58% Alice is surprising. Every election produces a surprising sample now and then, but less than 1 time in 20.

Bigger samples hug the box more tightly, which is why an auditor's sample can't be too small. But how big is big enough depends mostly on how close the election is, not on how many ballots were cast. A random sample of a few hundred ballots tells you nearly as much about a box of a million ballots as about a box of 1,000. A landslide shows up in a small sample; a close race needs a much bigger one before a tie starts to look surprising. The ballot polling page works out how big.

A surprising sample doesn't prove anything. As the figure shows, a draw from any election can be surprising now and then, just not very frequently. Ruling an election out is a decision, not a proof: we agree to stop taking seriously any election for which our sample would be surprising. The figure below draws that line at 1 in 20. Wherever the line goes, it comes with a cost. If the true election is one of the unlucky ones, bad luck will sometimes rule it out, about as often as the line allows. A stricter line, like 1 in 100, makes that mistake less often but needs more ballots before it can rule anything out. Audits draw a line like this too, called the risk limit, and the next part comes back to it.

Which elections could have produced this sample?

This box holds 1,000 ballots. The reported result is 600 votes for Alice and 400 for Bob, but the auditor can't see inside. Each draw adds one ballot to the sample. For every election the box could hold, from 0 votes for Alice to 1,000, the curve shows how surprising the sample would be, asked in whichever direction applies. The figure compares two ways to rule elections out, both wrong at most 1 time in 20. A two-sided test rules out elections on both sides of the sample, so it has to split that 1 in 20: it rules out an election when the sample would turn up less than 1 time in 40 in either direction. A one-sided test only asks whether an election could have given this many votes for Alice or more, so it can spend the whole 1 in 20 on that one side.

The vertical scale is stretched near the bottom so the small chances are easy to see: each step down is 10 times rarer. At first the box really does hold 600 to 400. Start over and draw a few ballots at a time. Left of the peak, the two tests share one curve; the only difference is the line. The one-sided test puts all of its 1 in 20 on the side that matters, so its line sits higher, and it reaches the tie first. In simulations of the 600–400 box, the two-sided test needed about a third more ballots, on average, before it first ruled out the tie. On the right, the two-sided test also rules out elections even better for Alice than the sample suggests, work an audit doesn't need. Statisticians call this curve a p-value function. The range the two-sided test leaves unshaded is a 95% confidence interval for what's in the box, and the point where the curve crosses the one-sided line is a 95% lower confidence bound: Alice received at least that many votes.

What's really in the box

Keep drawing and you may see an election get ruled out, then come back. That isn't a mistake in the figure. Each time the sample grows, the figure asks the question fresh, about the sample as it stands now. A short run of votes for Alice can push the tie below the line, and the next few ballots can lift it back up. Each snapshot is honest on its own. But imagine an auditor who checks after every ballot and stops the first time the tie is ruled out. Each check is another chance for a lucky run to push the tie below the line, and stopping early locks in whichever fluke comes first. If the election really was a tie, an auditor checking after each of the first 300 ballots would see it ruled out at some point about 1 time in 4, not 1 time in 20.

The result that matters

An audit doesn't need the exact count. It only needs to rule out the results in which the reported winner lost. The hardest of those to rule out is a tie, 500 to 500, because it sits closest to an Alice win. If an Alice-leaning sample would be rare from a tied box, it's rarer still from any box where Bob won. You can see this in the figure: left of its peak, the curve only rises, so once the tie drops below a line, every election Bob won is already below it. Making the case against the tie is enough.

That's why an audit uses the one-sided test. An election even better for Alice than the sample suggests isn't a problem, because Alice still won it, so there's no reason to spend any of the 1 in 20 ruling it out. Putting all of it on the side that matters lets the audit rule out the tie with fewer ballots.

It helps to be precise about what can go wrong. A risk-limiting audit only ever does one of two things: it confirms the reported outcome and stops, or it keeps going, all the way to a full hand count if it has to, and the hand count decides. The audit itself never declares a different winner. That leaves four possibilities:

The audit confirmsThe audit goes to a full hand count
The reported winner really wonRight, and cheapRight, but expensive: the hand count agrees with the reported result
The reported winner really lostThe one mistake that matters. Its chance is capped by the line, 1 in 20 hereRight: the hand count corrects the outcome

So an audit can't overturn a correct outcome. The worst it can do to one is cost a full hand count. The only harmful mistake is confirming an outcome that's wrong, and that's the chance the one-sided test keeps below its line. That chance is the audit's risk limit. Everything else is about efficiency: how often, and how soon, an audit confirms an outcome that's right.

One trap remains, the one the figure showed: an audit that checks after every ballot gets many chances to be fooled, so it can't simply stop the first time the sample looks rare. That raises the question the next part takes up: can an audit check as it goes, keep its risk limit, and use even fewer ballots? One way weighs the reported result against a tie, with what statisticians call a likelihood ratio. Others build on the one-sided tests from this page.
