Risk-limiting audits from the ground up · Part 1: sampling

What can a few ballots tell you?

After an election, officials report a winner. An audit checks that report against the paper ballots. A full hand count of the paper always finds the outcome the voters chose, but it means reading every ballot, which is slow and expensive. A risk-limiting audit reads a random sample of ballots instead, and stops as soon as the sample makes a strong enough case.

New to audits? Start with why audits check paper ballots.

This page is about the first half of that idea: why a random handful of ballots can speak for the whole box, and what it can and can't say.

An election, drawn as a picture

Every example on this page uses the simplest contest there is: two candidates, Alice and Bob, and each voter votes for one of them. Real ballots usually list many contests, and some contests let voters choose more than one candidate, so a ballot and a vote aren't the same thing. Here we follow just this one contest, and every ballot has exactly one vote in it. When the counting is done, the result is two numbers: how many votes Alice got, and how many Bob got.

Two numbers make a point on a grid, so we can draw any result as a place: Alice's count across, Bob's count up. Counting builds the picture one ballot at a time. Each ballot with a vote for Alice is one step to the right, and each ballot with a vote for Bob is one step up.

Counting ten ballots

Ten voters cast ten ballots. Count them one by one and watch where the steps lead. The diagonal splits the grid in two: below it Alice has more votes, above it Bob does, and on it the election is tied. Every ten-ballot result lands on the dashed line where the two counts add up to ten.

There is no order of voting

Counting in a different order draws a different path, but every path ends at the same result. Ten ballots, six with a vote for Alice and four with a vote for Bob, can be put in 210 different orders, and here they all are at once.

Every order of the same ten ballots

Each one of these paths represents the same election outcome: 6 for Alice, 4 for Bob. Lines are thicker where more orders pass, and the number at each point counts how many of the 210 orders go through it. They all start at (0, 0) and all end at (6, 4). The steps toward Alice tend to be thicker because she got more votes: 126 of the 210 orders start with a vote for Alice and only 84 with a vote for Bob. At every point, the orders leaving it split in proportion to the votes for Alice and for Bob still left to count.

So which of these paths is the election? None of them in particular, or all of them at once. Every one ends at the same result, and nothing about the election picks one out. It's tempting to picture an audit as replaying the election ballot by ballot, in the order people voted. There is no such order. Two people can mark their ballots at the same moment. Ballots from different polling places are handled in different rooms, and mail ballots arrive over weeks.

None of that matters to the audit. Once the polls close, what's left is a pile of paper whose count no longer changes. The auditor creates an order by taking ballots out one at a time. Every sequence on this page is an order of taking ballots out, never an order of voting.

If the auditor counted every ballot, the order still wouldn't matter: every path ends at the same result. But the whole point of an audit like this is to avoid reading every ballot where possible. An auditor who stops partway sees only the start of one path, and the start of a path can be far from where it ends. So the real question is whether the order the auditor sees can mislead them. It can, if the ballots aren't mixed first.

Mix first, then draw

To prevent getting an unclear picture of the election, auditors first shuffle the ballots. That way, it is less likely that they will see pockets of ballots that are less representative of the real outcome.

Humans are bad at randomness, so we need help to make sure we are really getting a good mix. Try it yourself.

Pick a number between 1 and 10

Go with your gut. Don't think about it too hard.

To make sure we are really getting a good mix, we can use sources of "true" randomness, like rolling a fair die. However, if we had to sample every ballot that way, it would be really painful.

To deal with this, we use computers to help. You might know that computers are really good at doing exactly the same thing every time, and really bad at doing different things every time. So we have special programs called "pseudo-random number generators" (PRNGs), which give a new number every time you ask them to. However, because computers are predictable (that's the "pseudo" part), these number generators are not really random, so they have to fake it. We help them out by giving them a kick with a truly random source, usually rolling dice to "seed" the PRNG.

Two generators, side by side

Each box below feeds a seed to the same pseudo-random number generator. Type anything you like as a seed: a word, your name, a number. The generator turns it into a stream of numbers between 1 and 10, like the ones you picked above. It works by scrambling the seed together with a counter (1, 2, 3, …) and turning each scrambled result into a number. Once the seed is chosen, nothing is random.

Guess the next number

This generator has a secret seed. It will show you its numbers one at a time; before each one, guess what it will be. Without the seed, you have nothing to go on.

In a real audit, then, the shuffle happens on paper rather than by shaking a box. Election offices keep a list of where every ballot is stored, called a ballot manifest, and use the PRNG's numbers to pick ballots from that list. In Colorado, for example, officials roll dice at a public meeting to make the seed. The public part matters. If anyone could predict which ballots would be picked, a mistake or a tampered batch could be kept out of the sample. Because the seed is rolled in the open after the ballots are already counted and listed, nobody could have known in advance where the auditor would look.

Drawing ballots one at a time doesn't make a sample less fair than grabbing a handful at once. Fifty ballots drawn one by one are just as random as fifty drawn together. Reading them in sequence only lets the audit stop early, as soon as it has seen enough.

Turning the question around

So far we knew what was in the box and asked what a sample would look like. An auditor has the opposite problem: they hold a sample and want to know what's in the box.

The same counting answers it, run backwards. For each box the election could have produced, ask how often a fair draw from that box gives exactly the sample in hand. Boxes that often produce it stay plausible. Boxes that almost never produce it start to look doubtful. Each possible box is checked on its own, so there's no need to guess beforehand which results were likely.

One ballot does tell you something; it just doesn't tell you much. As the sample grows, the curve narrows around boxes that look like the sample.

What's really in the box

The result that matters

An audit doesn't need the exact count. It only needs to rule out the results in which the reported winner lost. The hardest of those to rule out is a tie, 500 to 500, because it sits closest to an Alice win. If an Alice-leaning sample would be rare from a tied box, it's rarer still from any box where Bob won. Making the case against the tie is enough.

There are two natural ways to make that case. The first asks only how surprising the sample would be if the box were tied. Flip a coin 100 times, get 60 heads, and you'll doubt the coin without doing any math. The second also uses the reported result, and asks how much more often a 600–400 box would produce the sample than a tied one. Both lead to valid audits.

One trap catches both. An audit that checks after every ballot gets many chances to be fooled, so it can't simply stop the first time the sample looks rare. The next part shows how ballot polling audits handle that.
